1. Un dia normal: daily routine verbs

The whole unit is built around one question: Què fas cada dia? — “What do you do every day?”. Here is an ordinary weekday, told in the first person. The times use quarts; if they still confuse you, have a look at the lesson for unit 4.

  1. 7:00

    Em desperto a les set i em llevo de seguida.

    I wake up at seven and get up straight away.

  2. 7:15

    Em dutxo, em vesteixo i em rento les dents.

    I have a shower, get dressed and brush my teeth.

  3. 7:30

    A dos quarts de vuit esmorzo amb la meva parella.

    At half past seven I have breakfast with my partner.

  4. 8:30

    Agafo el metro i començo a treballar a dos quarts de nou.

    I take the metro and start work at half past eight.

  5. 14:00

    Dino amb els companys de feina.

    I have lunch with my colleagues.

  6. 17:00

    Plego a les cinc i torno a casa.

    I finish work at five and go back home.

  7. 18:00

    Els dimarts i els dijous vaig a classe de català.

    On Tuesdays and Thursdays I go to Catalan class.

  8. 21:00

    Sopo a les nou i després miro una sèrie o llegeixo.

    I have dinner at nine, then watch a series or read.

  9. 23:00

    Vaig a dormir a les onze.

    I go to bed at eleven.

Notice how much English does with “have” and “get” — have a shower, have lunch, get up, get dressed. Catalan uses one verb for each: dutxar-se, dinar, llevar-se, vestir-se.

False friends

Dinar is lunch, and dinner is sopar — an easy pair to swap. Both are verbs and nouns at once: Què hi ha per sopar? — “What’s for dinner?”. And plegar you’ll hear every evening in Barcelona: A quina hora plegues? — “What time do you finish work?”.

If you know Spanish

Llevar-se is levantarse — Spanish llevarse (“to take away”) is a false friend. Plegar is salir del trabajo, and dinar has nothing to do with dinero.

2. Em llevo, es dutxa: reflexive verbs

Half of the routine verbs are reflexive. In the dictionary they end in -se (llevar-se); in a sentence, a small pronoun goes before the verb and changes with the person. English has this only in a few verbs (I hurt myself), and for routine it never says “I shower myself” — but Catalan does. You already know one: Em dic Anna from the first lesson is dir-se, “to be called” (literally “to call oneself”).

llevar-sedutxar-seafaitar-se
joem llevoem dutxom'afaito
tuet lleveset dutxest'afaites
ell, ella, vostèes llevaes dutxas'afaita
nosaltresens llevemens dutxemens afaitem
vosaltresus lleveuus dutxeuus afaiteu
ells, elles, vostèses llevenes dutxens'afaiten

afaitar-se — to shave. It’s here to show what happens before a vowel.

  • The pronouns: em, et, es, ens, us, es — roughly “myself, yourself, himself/herself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves”. In English they come after the verb; in Catalan, before it: em dutxo — “(I) myself shower”.
  • Before a vowel or h, em, et, es lose their vowel and take an apostrophe: m'afaito, s'afaita. Ens and us don’t change: ens afaitem.
  • Don’t drop the pronoun. Llevo a les set is wrong — it has to be Em llevo a les set. Without the pronoun, llevar is done to someone else: Llevo els nens a les set. — I get the children up at seven.
  • Body parts take the article, not “my”: em rento les dents — “I brush my teeth”, literally “I wash myself the teeth”; es renta les mans — “she washes her hands”. Not les meves dents: the pronoun already says whose.
  • The same verb without the pronoun means doing it to someone or something else: Desperto els nens a les set. — I wake the children at seven. Rento els plats. — I wash the dishes.

2.1 After another verb

When another verb comes first (vull — I want, he de — I have to, vaig a — I’m going (somewhere) to…), the pronoun can go at the front or be attached to the infinitive with a hyphen. Either way it still changes with the person:

Demà vull llevar-me tard. = Demà em vull llevar tard. — Tomorrow I want to get up late.
Has de dutxar-te ara? — Do you have to shower now?

For English speakers

A rule of thumb: if the English verb is a routine you do to your own body — wake up, get up, shower, get dressed, shave, wash — the Catalan one is very likely reflexive. Learn them with -se on the end, and say them out loud with em in front: em desperto, em llevo, em dutxo, em vesteixo.

If you know Spanish

The pronouns look like Spanish me, te, se, nos, os, se, but the vowel comes first, and “you” plural is us, not os. The classic mistake is se dutxa — it’s es dutxa.

4. Amb qui? Who with, and on your own

Amb means “with”. It’s pronounced [əm]: the b is silent before a consonant but heard before a vowel: amb ell [əmˈbeʎ].

Amb qui dines? — Amb els companys de feina. — Who do you have lunch with? — With my colleagues.
Amb qui vas a passejar? — Amb el meu gos. — Who do you go for walks with? — With my dog.
La Marta va a l'escola amb el seu pare. — Marta goes to school with her dad.
En Joan juga a futbol amb la seva germana. — Joan plays football with his sister.

Notice the word order: English puts “with” at the end (Who do you go with?), Catalan puts it first (Amb qui vas?). And for someone else’s relatives it’s el seu, la seva, with the article, like el meu (lesson for unit 2, chapter 4). It agrees with the relative, not the owner: Joan’s sister is la seva germana.

4.1 Amb mi, amb tu: with me, with you

After amb — and after any preposition — “me” is mi, not jo. The others look just like the subject pronouns:

EnglishCatalan
with meamb mi
with youamb tu
with him, with heramb ell, amb ella
with you (polite)amb vostè
with usamb nosaltres
with you (plural)amb vosaltres
with themamb ells, amb elles

Vens al cinema amb mi? — Are you coming to the cinema with me?
Sí, vaig amb tu! — Yes, I’ll go with you!

Mistake

amb jo is wrong — after a preposition it’s always mi, just as English says “with me”, not “with I”. But amb tu is correct: “you” has no special form.

If you know Spanish

There is no conmigo or contigo in Catalan: just amb mi, amb tu.

Els dissabtes sopem junts. — On Saturdays we have dinner together.
La Laia i la Mar estudien juntes. — Laia and Mar study together.

In English, “alone” and “together” never change. In Catalan they’re adjectives: they agree with the people in gender and number. Two women are juntes; Elles estudien junts is a mistake.

  • Gairebé means “almost”: gairebé sempre, gairebé mai, gairebé cada dia.
  • Word order is freer than in English. English puts “often” before the verb (I often read); Catalan usually puts these words after the verb or at the start: Llegeixo sovint, De vegades llegeixo.

5.1 Mai and no: the double negative

After the verb, mai needs no before the verb — a double negative that’s perfectly correct in Catalan:

No bereno mai. — I never have an afternoon snack (literally “I don’t snack never”).
No miro mai la tele. — I never watch TV.

At the start of the sentence there are two options: Mai no bereno. is more formal and preferred in writing; Mai bereno. is also correct and more common in speech. What’s wrong is mai after the verb without no: Bereno mai.

For English speakers

“I don’t never…” sounds wrong in standard English, so the temptation is Bereno mai. In Catalan it’s the other way round: without no the sentence is broken. Think of mai as “ever” that needs a “not”: No bereno mai — “I don’t ever snack”.

  • Un cop and una vegada mean the same thing, “one time”. Catalan has no single word for “once” or “twice”: it’s always “one time, two times”. Cop is masculine (dos cops), vegada feminine (dues vegades).
  • The unit title is Estudio català els dimarts i els dijous: with the article, a day means “every week”, like the English plural “on Tuesdays”. Both els dimarts and el dimarts can mean “on Tuesdays”. Without the article, dimarts is “this coming Tuesday”: Ens veiem dimarts. — See you on Tuesday. And there’s no a before days: als dimarts is a mistake. More on days of the week in the lesson for unit 4.

Cada quan vas al gimnàs? — Dos cops per setmana, els dilluns i els dijous. — How often do you go to the gym? — Twice a week, on Mondays and Thursdays.
Quan fas videotrucades amb la teva família? — Els diumenges, gairebé sempre. — When do you video-call your family? — On Sundays, almost always.

Cada quan…? — “How often…?”, literally “every when” (Cada quant de temps…? is also correct). Don’t mix up quan (“when”) and quant (“how much”): they sound the same, but they’re spelt differently.

6. Anar a: where to and what for

“To go” is anar: vaig, vas, va, anem, aneu, van. “To” a place is a, which merges with the masculine article:

a +BecomesExample
elalVaig al mercat.
elsalsAnem als Pirineus.
laa laVa a la piscina.
l'a l'Van a l'escola.
no articlea casa, a classeTorno a casa.

Never say a el: the merger is compulsory, in speech and in writing. a la and a l' stay separate.

6.1 Anar a + infinitive: going somewhere to do something

Vaig a comprar pa. — I’m going (out) to buy bread.
La Laia va a comprar amb la seva mare. — Laia goes shopping with her mum.
Els dissabtes anem a passejar a la platja. — On Saturdays we go for a walk on the beach.

Trap: vaig a and vaig

Vaig a comprar pa — “I’m going to buy bread”: present tense, you’re on your way. Vaig comprar pa without a — “I bought bread”: the past (passat perifràstic, introductory lesson, section 3.1). One letter changes the tense!

And don’t use vaig a for the English future “I’m going to…” when nobody is going anywhere. Use the present with a time word: Demà compro pa. — I’m going to buy bread tomorrow.

6.2 Anar and venir: “I’m coming!”

Venir (vinc, vens, ve…) is movement towards the place where the speaker is (“come here”), or together with the speaker: Vens amb mi? — Are you coming with me? Everything else is anar — including going towards the person you’re talking to, where English says “come”:

Vine! — Ja vaig! — Come here! — I’m coming!
Vens a sopar a casa meva dissabte? — Are you coming to dinner at my place on Saturday? (the host is speaking)
Demà vaig a casa teva. — I’ll come to your place tomorrow.

For English speakers

This is the big one. English “I’m coming” means “I’m on my way to you”, so learners say Ja vinc!. You will hear it, but standard Catalan (and your teacher) wants Ja vaig!: you are going away from where you are. Ask yourself: is someone moving to where the speaker is right now, or going along with the speaker? Only then venir.

One colloquial phrase to learn as a whole: Ara vinc! — “I’ll be right back (here)!”, said when you step out for a moment.

If you know Spanish

Here Catalan works exactly like Spanish: Ja vaig! = ¡Ya voy!

Write eight to ten sentences about your usual day and week: when you get up, who you eat with, when you finish work or classes, what you do once a week, what you never do. Use at least three reflexive verbs and three expressions of frequency.

Example

Em llevo a un quart de vuit. Em dutxo i esmorzo sol, perquè la meva dona es lleva més tard. Treballo en una oficina a prop de casa i hi vaig a peu. Dino amb els companys a les dues. Plego a les sis. Els dimarts i els dijous vaig a classe de català. Dos cops per setmana faig esport. Els diumenges faig una videotrucada amb els meus pares. No miro mai la tele, però de vegades miro una sèrie amb la meva dona. Vaig a dormir a les dotze.

Mission

Tell a classmate about your usual day and week: Em llevo a… Dino amb… Un cop per setmana… No … mai. Then ask about theirs (A quina hora et lleves? Amb qui dines? Què fas el cap de setmana?) and report their answers in the third person: En Pau es lleva a les sis…
