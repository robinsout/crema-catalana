1. Aliments: what's in the basket

Many food words are easy to guess through English or French (el iogurt, la xocolata, el tomàquet). What takes work is three things: the words that look like nothing you know, the gender of each noun (it's in the article, so learn every word with el or la), and the false friends that could leave you buying the wrong thing.

  • Gender you can't guess: la llimona and la mantega are feminine, el formatge and l'enciam masculine. Els llegums (pulses) is masculine. Les postres (dessert) is feminine and always plural: De postres, fruita. — For dessert, fruit. Before a vowel el and la usually both become l', so l'all, l'oli, l'ou hide their gender — check the dictionary or the plural (els ous).
  • One word for the whole bunch: English says grapes, plural; Catalan el raïm is singular, like English fruit: Vull un quilo de raïm. — I'd like a kilo of grapes. A single grape is un gra de raïm. La fruita, la verdura (vegetables), la carn (meat) and el peix work the same way.
  • El pernil is ham in general: pernil salat is cured ham (the dark, thinly sliced kind), pernil dolç is cooked ham, the sandwich kind.
  • L'oli with no other word means olive oil in Catalonia. Sunflower oil is oli de gira-sol.

False friends

El raïm is grapes, not raisins — a raisin is una pansa. Els caramels are sweets (boiled sweets), not caramel. La fruita seca means nuts (les nous, les avellanes, les ametlles — walnuts, hazelnuts, almonds), not dried fruit, which is fruita dessecada. El pebrot is the vegetable (bell pepper), el pebre the spice: English uses one word, Catalan two. La pasta is pasta, but les pastes in the plural are small biscuits or pastries to have with coffee.

Pronunciation

Formatge is [fuɾˈmad͡ʒə]: tg sounds like the dg in bridge, and the unstressed o becomes [u]; compare French fromage. Raïm is [rəˈim], two syllables, ra-eem: the two dots on ï stop ai from merging into one sound as in English rhyme. Ametlla is [əˈmeʎːə]: tll is a long [ʎ], the lli sound of million, held a moment longer. Llet and lluç start with that same [ʎ].

If you know Spanish

Most food words are close to Spanish, but watch the gender: la llimona (el limón), els llegums (las legumbres), les postres (el postre). El raïm is singular where Spanish says las uvas. And la mantega is butter (mantequilla), not manteca — lard is el llard. La fruita seca is frutos secos, nuts.

2. Fruita i verdura de temporada: seasonal produce

At a Barcelona market the stalls change with the seasons, and the stallholder will happily tell you what's good right now: Ara és temporada de maduixes. — Strawberries are in season now. Seasonal food is cheaper and tastier, so this list is useful well beyond the lesson.

  • Winter (a l'hivern): oranges, mandarins, la carxofa — artichoke, la coliflor — cauliflower, les bledes — chard, and els calçots, a special mild spring onion grilled over a fire in winter and spring (more in chapter 7).
  • Spring (a la primavera): strawberries, la cirera — cherry, la nespra — loquat, els pèsols — peas, els espàrrecs — asparagus, les faves — broad beans.
  • Summer (a l'estiu): watermelon, el meló — melon, el préssec — peach, l'albercoc — apricot, l'albergínia — aubergine (the British word comes from the same Arabic root), el carbassó — courgette.
  • Autumn (a la tardor): grapes, la magrana — pomegranate, el caqui — persimmon (kaki), la carbassa — pumpkin, mushrooms, les castanyes — chestnuts.

Keep the pair apart: el carbassó is the small one (courgette), la carbassa the big one (pumpkin) — here -ó is a "small" ending: carbassó is literally "little pumpkin", just as courgette is French for "little courge".

For English speakers

“In winter” is a l'hivern: the preposition is a, and the season keeps its article — literally “in the winter”: a l'hivern, a la primavera, a l'estiu, a la tardor. Don't drop the article as English does. Months and seasons are covered in the lesson Numbers and calendar, chapter 7.

If you know Spanish

Several seasonal words differ from Spanish: la carxofa (alcachofa), les bledes (acelgas), la nespra (níspero), els pèsols (guisantes), el préssec (melocotón), el carbassó and la carbassa (calabacín, calabaza), la magrana (granada).

3. Què vols? Vull… The verb voler

Voler means “to want”. It's irregular, and the forms you need most in a shop are worth learning by heart:

voler
jovull
tuvols
ell, ella, vostèvol
nosaltresvolem
vosaltresvoleu
ells, elles, vostèsvolen

Què vols? — Vull cireres. — What do you want? — I want cherries.
Què vol, senyora? — Vull mig quilo de lluç. — What would you like, madam? — Half a kilo of hake.
Què voleu per sopar? — Volem truita de patates. — What do you (all) want for dinner? — We want potato omelette.
Vull comprar fruita. — I want to buy some fruit.

  • Vull is [ˈbuʎ]. Two surprises for English ears: in Barcelona v is pronounced like b, and the final ll is the lli of million, not a plain l. All the other forms keep the stem vol-: only the “I” form is irregular.
  • No do in the question: Què vols? is just “what want-you?”.
  • An infinitive follows directly, with no “to”: Vull comprar — I want to buy.
  • “Some” disappears: Vull fruita. — I want some fruit; Vull cireres. — I want some cherries. Add the article only when you mean particular ones: Vull les pomes vermelles. — I'd like the red apples.

More polite: voldria

In English “I want a kilo of apples” sounds blunt at a counter. In Catalan Vull un quilo de pomes is perfectly normal and not rude at all. Still, you'll often hear softer versions too. Learn them as set phrases:

Voldria un quilo de pomes. — I'd like a kilo of apples.
Em posa mig quilo de tomàquets? — Could I have half a kilo of tomatoes? (literally “do you put me…?”)
Em dona una barra de pa, si us plau? — Could I have a baguette, please? (literally “do you give me…?”)

If you know Spanish

Voler is querer, voldria is quería / querría, and Em posa…? is the same counter phrase as ¿Me pone…?

4. A la parada: tu or vostè

The conversation at a stall is almost always the same. Learn it whole, and you can do your shopping in Catalan from the first word to the last.

4.1 The queue

At the market and in small shops there's often no ticket machine and no orderly queue: people stand around the stall, and the queue is kept by one question. When you arrive, ask who's last:

Qui és l'últim? — Jo. — Who's last? — Me.
Qui és l'última? — Soc jo. — Who's last? (if the last person is probably a woman) — Me.

Remember who you're after and don't miss your turn: Ara em toca a mi. — It's my turn now. The stallholder will ask: A qui li toca? — Whose turn is it?

4.2 The whole conversation

WhoLineEnglish

  • Què li poso? — literally “what do I put (in the bag) for you?”, the standard “what can I get you?”. Speaking to you as tu: Què et poso? You'll also simply hear Què vol? / Què vols?
  • A quant van les maduixes? — “how much are the strawberries (per kilo)?”, for things sold by weight. For things sold by the piece: Quant val? / Quant valen? or Quant costa?
  • Prices: cinc euros amb vint or, shorter, cinc vint; vint cèntims — twenty cents. Euro is [ˈɛwɾu], two syllables, not “you-roh”. Numbers up to a thousand are in the lesson Numbers and calendar, chapter 2.
  • Pagar amb targeta — to pay by card, en efectiu — in cash. You ask Puc pagar amb targeta?, or the seller asks first: Amb targeta o en efectiu? Change is el canvi.

4.3 Res: “anything” and “nothing”

An odd-looking pair: the seller asks Res més? and you answer Res més — the very same words. That's because res means “anything” in a question and “nothing” in an answer, so it covers both halves of English Anything else? — Nothing else:

Vol res més? — Would you like anything else?
No, res més, gràcies. — No, nothing else, thanks.
Ja està, gràcies. — That's all, thanks.

In a full sentence res needs no before the verb: No vull res — I don't want anything (literally “I don't want nothing”). Catalan requires this double negative where standard English forbids it. The other question, Alguna cosa més?, uses alguna cosa — “something”. Both questions mean the same.

4.4 Tu and vostè at the counter

English has one “you”; Catalan has two. At a Barcelona market, sellers often use tu straight away, especially with younger people and regulars; with older customers, vostè. Just answer the way you're spoken to. Vostè takes the “he/she” form of the verb — rather like the old-fashioned waiter's “What would madam like?”:

MeaningWith tuWith vostè
What would you like?Què vols?Què vol?
What can I get you?Què et poso?Què li poso?
Anything else?Vols res més?Vol res més?

Tingui and té

Tingui is the vostè command form of tenir (“to have”), literally “have!”: it's what you say when you hand over money or goods — “here you are”. With tu it's té, spelt with the same accent as té “he has”. Don't mix it up with el te (no accent) — tea.

If you know Spanish

Vostè works like usted, Què li poso? is ¿Qué le pongo?, and tingui / té are tenga / ten. Res més? is both ¿algo más? and nada más.

5. Quant? Un quilo, mig quilo, una dotzena

5.1 Quant, quanta, quants, quantes

English already splits “how much” (things you don't count) from “how many” (things you count). Catalan does the same with singular and plural — and on top of that, quant agrees with the noun's gender:

how much (singular)how many (plural)
masculineQuant lluç vols?Quants cigrons vols?
feminineQuanta carn picada vols?Quantes mandarines vols?
  • Singular for what you don't count piece by piece (lluç, carn, formatge, farina), plural for what you count (cigrons, ous, pomes) — the same line English draws between how much cheese and how many eggs.
  • Then choose by gender: carn is feminine → quanta; mandarines feminine plural → quantes.
  • For a price or a total it's quant: Quant és? — How much is it? Quant costa? — How much does it cost? But when the product is understood without being named, quant still agrees with it: Quantes en vol? — How many would you like? (about mandarins).
  • The answer is a quantity: Un quilo. Mig quilo. Tres o quatre. Una dotzena.

Quantes en vol?

At the counter you'll hear Quantes en vol? — “How many (of them) would you like?” and Me'n posa mig quilo. — “Give me half a kilo (of them)”. The little word en stands for the product already mentioned, like “of them” or “some” in English — and exactly like French en in j'en veux deux, if you know French. It's explained properly in later units; for now just recognise it.

When you buy more than one, remember that “two” has gender in Catalan: dos pots, dos paquets, but dues llaunes, dues ampolles. English two never changes, so dues is easy to forget.

Pot, bric, bossa

Un pot is a jar — honey, jam, chickpeas in a glass jar — not a cooking pot, which is una olla (as in carn d'olla, chapter 7). Un bric is a drinks carton (from the Tetra Brik brand). Una garrafa is a big plastic water bottle, not a carafe. A carrier bag at the till is una bossa: Vol bossa? — Do you need a bag? Bags cost money in Catalonia, so people go to the market with their own bag or a shopping trolley — el carro de la compra.

If you know Spanish

Mig / mitja are medio / media, un pot is un bote, un tarro, una llauna is una lata, una ampolla is una botella, una capsa is una caja. And Spanish dos has no feminine: dues llaunes, dos latas.

6. On ho compres? Shops and shopkeepers

Small shops are alive and well in Barcelona, and each one is named after what it sells, with the shopkeeper named on the same pattern. The scheme is simple: -eria is the shop, -er / -era the person who works there — like English baker — bakery, butcher. (Feminine job titles are in the unit 6 lesson, chapter 1.)

  • El forn is both “oven” and “bakery”: Compro el pa al forn de la cantonada. — I buy bread at the bakery on the corner. La fleca is the same thing, a touch more formal.
  • In conversation people often name the shopkeeper instead of the shop, with cal (= casa del, “the house of”; a cal carnisser = a casa del carnisser) — just like British “to the butcher's”: Vaig a cal carnisser. — I'm going to the butcher's.

I la carn? — Al supermercat. És més barata. — And meat? — At the supermarket. It's cheaper.

Barcelona's markets

A municipal market (el mercat municipal) is not an open-air farmers' market but a covered hall with permanent stalls, and almost every neighbourhood in Barcelona has one. Tourists know la Boqueria, but locals shop at their own neighbourhood market — el mercat de Sant Antoni, de l'Abaceria, de Santa Caterina. They open from early morning until about lunchtime, and on Mondays many stalls are closed (there's hardly any fish on a Monday).

7. La cuina a Catalunya: food through the year

Catalan cooking has its own calendar: almost every festival has its dish or sweet, sold only in its season. This calendar also revises the months and festivals.

  • L'escudella i carn d'olla is the great winter dish: a broth (l'escudella) with big shell-shaped pasta called galets, followed by the meat and vegetables cooked in it, served separately (la carn d'olla, “pot meat”). Cold weather — fa fred — means it's time for escudella.
  • El tortell de Reis (Twelfth Night, 6 January) is a ring of sweet bread with surprises inside: a little king figure and a dried bean. Whoever gets the king wears the paper crown; whoever gets the bean (la fava) pays for the next tortell.
  • Before Easter, in Lent (la Quaresma), people eat bunyols — small doughnuts — and lots of bacallà. At Easter, godparents (els padrins) give their godchildren la mona — a cake or a chocolate figure.
  • La crema catalana is a custard with a crunchy burnt-sugar top, like crème brûlée. It's also called crema de Sant Josep: by tradition you eat it on 19 March.
  • On Saint John's Eve (la revetlla de Sant Joan, 23 June) people light bonfires and fireworks and eat coca — a flat sweet cake topped with candied fruit and pine nuts — with a glass of cava.
  • At la Castanyada (31 October – 1 November) people eat roast chestnuts (castanyes), baked sweet potatoes (moniatos) and panellets — little balls of almond paste, often rolled in pine nuts. It's the local alternative to Halloween.

Every day

All year round there's pa amb tomàquet: bread rubbed with a ripe tomato, with olive oil and salt. And the classic children's afternoon snack (el berenar, see the unit 4 lesson, chapter 2) is pa amb xocolata: bread with a bar of chocolate.

Mission

Do one shopping trip at the market entirely in Catalan: ask who's last (Qui és l'últim?), order two or three things with quantities (Voldria mig quilo de…, una dotzena d'…), ask the price (A quant van…?), answer Alguna cosa més? and ask Quant és? If the stallholder hears your accent and switches to Spanish or English, politely carry on in Catalan — they usually switch back.
