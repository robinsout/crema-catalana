1. Who’s this? People by age

The unit opens with Qui és aquesta persona? — “Who is this person?”. The first answer you need is “a baby, a girl, an old man”:

AgeMaleFemaleNote
babyun nadóun nadónadó is masculine even for a baby girl; also un bebè, una criatura
childun nenuna nenaboy, girl (small)
teenager, young personun noiuna noiaboy, girl, “guy”: used for thirty-year-olds too
adultun homeuna donathe h is silent: [ˈɔmə]
elderlyun home granuna dona granpolite: literally a “big” man or woman
  • Every Catalan noun is either masculine or feminine, and the word for “a” shows it: un (masculine), una (feminine). For people, the gender usually follows the person: un nen, una nena.
  • Gran means “big” for things (una casa gran) and “old” or “older” for people: el meu germà gran — my older brother. The word vell (old) sounds blunt about people.
  • La criatura is feminine even when it’s a boy: en Guillem és una criatura. Children as a group are often la canalla or la mainada (“the kids”): collective nouns, used in the singular — la canalla és…
  • Plural: els nens — “children” in general, boys and girls together (more on this in chapter 2).

Qui és? — És un noi. Es diu David. — Who’s that? — It’s a young man. His name is David.
Qui són? — Són una dona gran i un nen. — Who are they? — An old woman and a little boy.

Watch out

Una dona is both “a woman” and “a wife”. Un noi is “a boy, a guy”, while “new” is nou. And home is “man”, not “home”!

2. Family: who’s who

Most family words come in pairs: the masculine often ends in a consonant or -e, the feminine in -a. Learn each with its article: el (masculine), la (feminine), l' before a vowel, els / les in the plural.

MasculineFemininePlural (both)

el germàla germanaels germans

el cunyatla cunyadaels cunyats

In English: father — mother — parents; son — daughter — children; brother — sister; grandfather — grandmother; grandson — granddaughter; uncle — aunt; cousin; nephew — niece; father-in-law — mother-in-law; brother-in-law — sister-in-law; son-in-law — daughter-in-law.

  • The masculine plural covers mixed groups. English has separate words for “parents”, “siblings”, “children”, “grandparents”; Catalan just uses the masculine plural: els pares — parents (father + mother), els germans — brothers and sisters, els fills — sons and daughters, els avis — grandparents. So Tinc tres germans may well include sisters.
  • l'oncle but la tia: two different words, just like English uncle — aunt. In everyday Barcelona speech you’ll constantly hear the affectionate el tiet, la tieta.
  • el net — grandson. It has a twin: net, neta also means “clean”: una casa neta.
  • cosí has a masculine and a feminine form: el cosí, la cosina. English cousin doesn’t tell you which.
  • germà has an accent, but germana and germans don’t: a stressed final syllable gets the mark only if the word ends in a vowel, in -as, -es, -is, -os, -us or in -en, -in. Germans ends in -ns, and in germana the stress is on the second-to-last syllable. Say [ʒərˈma]: g before e is the sound in measure.
  • fill is [ˈfiʎ]: ll sounds like the lli in million.

For English speakers

In-laws work just like in English: your spouse’s parents are els sogres (parents-in-law); your spouse’s brothers and sisters, and your siblings’ husbands and wives, are els cunyats (brothers- and sisters-in-law). Your daughter’s husband is el gendre, your son’s wife la nora.

3. Aquest, aquesta: “this” changes for gender

Pointing at a photo and saying who it is — that’s the core of the unit. In English, this never changes; in Catalan it agrees with the person or thing in gender and number:

singularplural
masc.aquestaquests
fem.aquestaaquestes

Aquest és el meu pare. — This is my father.
Aquesta és la meva germana, la Núria. — This is my sister, Núria.
Aquests són els meus avis. — These are my grandparents.
Aquestes són les meves cosines. — These are my (female) cousins.

  • English says “this is my father” and “this is my sister” with the same this; Catalan says aquest for a man and aquesta for a woman.
  • Pointing at yourself in a photo works the same way: a woman says Aquesta soc jo., a man Aquest soc jo. (“This is me”). Note the verb agrees with jo: literally “this am I”, not “this is me”.
  • Pronunciation: in aquest and aquests the s before t usually disappears in speech: “a-KET”, “a-KETS”.
  • With a noun: aquest home, aquesta noia, aquests nens.
  • For things further away there is aquell, aquella — “that”.

If you know Spanish

Spanish has three steps: este, ese, aquel. Central Catalan has two: aquest covers este and nearly always ese; aquell is aquel. So ese chico in conversation is usually aquest noi.

3.1 Asking about people

Qui és aquest noi? — És el meu germà. — Who’s this guy? — My brother.
Com es diu? — Es diu Marc. — What’s his name? — Marc.
Qui són? — Són els meus sogres. — Who are they? — My parents-in-law.
Com es diuen? — Es diuen Jaume i Rosa. — What are their names? — Jaume and Rosa.

Qui (who) doesn’t change: Qui és? and Qui són? The verb shows singular or plural, as in English who is / who are.

4. Our, your, their; “my” in the plural

In unit 1 you met el meu, el teu, el seu (lesson 1, chapter 5). Talking about family you need plurals: “my children”, “our daughter”, “their parents”. Here is the full table. Remember: the article comes first (“the my”), and the form agrees with the thing owned, not with the owner.

els meus avis — my grandparents

Els meus cunyats i els seus fills viuen a Reus. — My brother-in-law, his wife and their children live in Reus.

  • The article is always there, in the plural too: not meus avis but els meus avis.
  • English “your” is one word; Catalan splits it: el teu to one friend, el vostre to several.
  • el seu means “his”, “her”, “their” and polite “your”. When it’s unclear whose, use de + name: la mare d'en Pau, el marit de la Laia (chapter 5).
  • One exception to learn now: “at my place” is a casa meva, a casa teva, a casa nostra — no article, possessive after the noun. You’ll need it in Bàsic 2 (Com és casa teva?).

5. Whose? “Marta’s son”: de + personal article

Catalan has no ’s. “Marta’s son” is “the son of Marta” — and since names take a personal article in Catalan (lesson 1, chapter 2.2), it’s “the son of the Marta”. De shortens only before en (d'en); before la and l' it stays whole:

Articlede + articleExample
en Martíd'enla germana d'en Martí
la Martade lael fill de la Marta
l'Anna, l'Oriolde l'els avis de l'Anna

En Biel és el fill de la Laia i d'en Pau. — Biel is Laia and Pau’s son.
La Clara és la neboda de l'Oriol. — Clara is Oriol’s niece.
El meu sogre és el pare de la meva dona. — My father-in-law is my wife’s father.

  • Order is the reverse of English: first the relative, then the person — la germana d'en Martí, “the sister of Martí”.
  • Don’t mix up d'en (before a man’s name) and del (de + el, before a noun with el): la casa d'en Jordi but la casa del meu pare.
  • In everyday Barcelona speech you’ll also hear el Jordi, and so del Jordi. In class and in writing: d'en Jordi.

6. On viu la teva mare? The verb viure

Where your relatives live is the unit’s second big question. You know visc from lesson 1; now here’s the whole verb. Catalan verbs have six forms, and the ending tells you who — so the pronoun (jo, tu, ell…) is usually dropped.

viureexample
joviscVisc a Gràcia.
tuviusOn vius?
ell, ella, vostèviuLa meva mare viu a Dublín.
nosaltresvivimVivim a prop de la platja.
vosaltresviviuOn viviu?
ells, elles, vostèsviuenEls meus avis viuen a Edimburg.
  • viu, vius, viuen have u as a glide, like the end of English now: viu [ˈbiw] is one syllable, viuen [ˈbiwən] two. The v sounds like [b] in central Catalan.
  • “To live in” is viure a: Viu a Tarragona, Viuen a Escòcia.
  • “To live with someone” is viure amb: Visc amb la meva parella. “Alone” is sol, sola: El meu germà viu sol.

On viu la teva germana? — Viu a Londres, amb el seu marit. — Where does your sister live? — In London, with her husband.
I els teus pares? — Viuen a Manchester. — And your parents? — In Manchester.

No do/does in questions: just the question word and the verb, On vius? — “Where live-you?”.

Some city names have Catalan forms: Londres (London), Nova York, Edimburg (Edinburgh), Dublín, Saragossa (Zaragoza).

  • tinc ends in -c in the “I” form, like visc (introduction, chapter 5.1).
  • té has an accent: without it, te means “you” (object) or “tea”.
  • Age is something you have, not something you are: Tinc trenta-dos anys. — “I have thirty-two years”. Not soc trenta-dos. In a full sentence keep anys; a short answer can be just the number: Quants anys tens? — Vint-i-nou.

Quants anys tens? — Tinc vint-i-nou anys. — How old are you? — Twenty-nine.
Quants anys té la teva mare? — Té seixanta-quatre anys. — How old is your mum? — Sixty-four.
Quants germans tens? — Tinc un germà i dues germanes. — How many brothers and sisters have you got? — One brother and two sisters.
No tinc germans. Soc fill únic. — I haven’t got any brothers or sisters. I’m an only child (a woman says filla única).

In short answers you’ll often hear en: Tens fills? — Sí, en tinc dos. — “Have you got children? — Yes, I’ve got two (of them)”. This en is a pronoun meaning “of them”; for now, just recognise it.

7.1 Numbers from 21 to 99

Numbers are covered in detail in Els nombres i el calendari. For ages, the key points:

NumberWrittenWatch out
21–29vint-i-u, vint-i-dos… vint-i-nouonly the twenties have -i- (“and”)
31–99trenta-u, quaranta-sis, noranta-noujust a hyphen, as in English forty-six

  • Before a noun, u becomes un: vint-i-un anys, quaranta-un anys, not vint-i-u anys.
  • Numbers ending in 1 and 2 have a feminine form: vint-i-una alumnes, trenta-dues persones. Any (year) is masculine, so ages always use un, dos.
  • vint on its own sounds like “bin”, but in vint-i-dos you hear the t: “bin-tee-DOS”.

7.2 També and tampoc: “me too” and “me neither”

Tinc dos germans. — Jo també. — I’ve got two brothers. — Me too.
No tinc fills. — Jo tampoc. — I haven’t got children. — Me neither.
No tinc fills. — Jo sí. — I haven’t got children. — I have.
Tinc un gos. — Jo no. — I’ve got a dog. — I haven’t.

For English speakers

This works exactly like English: agreeing with a positive — també (me too); agreeing with a negative — tampoc (me neither); disagreeing — jo sí / jo no (I do / I don’t). Catalan usually doesn’t repeat the verb the way English does (I have, I haven’t). Careful: No tinc fills. — Jo també. is a mistake — after a negative it’s Jo tampoc, or Jo sí if you do have children.

8. Catalan names and two surnames

In class, in the photos and in the unit’s audio you’ll hear Catalan first names. Some can fool you:

NameWhoNote
Joana man!= John; the woman’s name is Joana
Paua man= Paul; also the word for “peace”
Jordia man= George
Jaumea man= James
Biela manshort for Gabriel
Xaviera mansaid “sha-bee-EH”: the final r is silent
Mara woman“sea”; often from Maria del Mar
Montsea womanfrom Montserrat, like the mountain and monastery
Núria, Laia, Mireia, Meritxellwomentypically Catalan names
Neusa woman“snows” (from Our Lady of the Snows)

Everyone has two surnames: usually the first is the father’s and the second the mother’s (parents can choose the order). Adrià Roca Pons is the son of Mr Roca and Ms Pons. Traditionally the two are joined with i (“and”): Roca i Pons.

For English speakers

Women don’t take their husband’s surname. If the mother is Elvira Puig and the father Pere Soler, the children are Soler Puig, and Elvira stays Puig all her life. That’s why forms ask for primer cognom and segon cognom (first and second surname); with a single English surname, you just leave the second one blank.
