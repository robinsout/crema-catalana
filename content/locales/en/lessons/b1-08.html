1. És alta, té els ulls verds, porta ulleres: three verbs for looks

Describing a person in Catalan rests on three verbs. English splits things in a similar way — “she is tall”, “she has green eyes”, “he wears glasses” — so the system will feel familiar. The surprises are in the details: Catalan uses “wear” for more things than English does, and where English says “a man with a beard”, Catalan often prefers a verb or de.

VerbWhat it describesExampleEnglish
ser (to be)what the person is like: height, build, hair and skin colour, age, characterÉs alta i morena.She's tall and dark-haired.
tenir (to have)what the person has by nature: eyes, hair, skin, age in yearsTé els ulls verds i els cabells llisos.She has green eyes and straight hair.
portar (to wear)what the person wears or has chosen: clothes, glasses, earrings, beard, hairstylePorta ulleres i barba.He wears glasses and has a beard.
  • A beard and a moustache are also portar: you “wear” them, because you can shave them off. Té barba is correct too, but in descriptions you'll mostly hear porta barba i bigoti. No article: porta ulleres, porta barba — “wears glasses”, “wears beard”.
  • Being bald is a feature, not a choice, so it's ser: És calb, és calba — he's bald, she's bald.
  • The adjective changes with the person: alt for a man, alta for a woman. English adjectives never change; Catalan ones do it all the time — more in chapter 3.

For English speakers

English loves “with”: “a girl with a plait”, “the man with glasses”. Amb works too (un home amb barba), but in descriptions Catalan more often uses a verb: una noia que porta una trena — a girl who wears a plait; or, shorter, de: la noia de la trena — the girl with the plait. That de + feature is very handy when you're pointing someone out: el noi de les ulleres — the boy with the glasses, la senyora del mocador — the lady in the headscarf.

If you know Spanish

The three verbs are Spanish ser, tener, llevar, used the same way. So portar is llevar: Porta un jersei vermell — lleva un jersey rojo. Catalan llevar means something else entirely (“to remove, take away”), and llevar-se is “to get up” (lesson 5).

1.2 És prima or està prima?

Catalan has two verbs for “to be”. Ser says what someone is like in general; estar says how they are now, what they've become, what state they're in:

La Marta és prima. — Marta is slim (that's how she is).
La Marta està molt prima. — Marta has got very thin, she's looking very thin.
El meu avi està una mica grassonet. — My grandad has put on a bit of weight.
Tots estan contents. — Everyone's happy.
Avui estàs molt moreno! — You're really tanned today!

That last one is worth remembering: és moreno — he's dark-haired, dark-skinned; està moreno — he's got a tan. English often marks the difference with “has got”, “is looking”: when you'd say that, think estar.

2. Els cabells i els ulls: hair, eyes, hairstyle

In English “hair” is uncountable and singular: her hair is long. In Catalan it's usually plural: té els cabells llargs — literally “she has the hairs long”. The adjective is plural too: llargs, llisos, rossos. The collective singular té el cabell llarg is also correct and you'll hear it, but the plural is the textbook's main pattern — and the one to learn first.

Note also the article: té els ulls verds — “has the eyes green”. With body parts Catalan uses el, la, els, les where English says “her”, “his” or nothing.

Pronunciation

Cabells — [kəˈβeʎs]: ll as in million, and the s after it is pronounced. The same in ulls [ˈuʎs] — eyes, and ulleres [uˈʎeɾəs] — glasses (from ull, eye).

2.1 Hair colour: what the person is

Hair colour is often an adjective about the person, with ser — like English “she's blonde”, “he's a redhead”:

  • Ros is “blond”, not “rose” (“pink” is rosa). A redhead is pèl-roig, literally “red-haired” (el pèl — a hair, fur; roig — red).
  • Brown hair is castany (“chestnut”), not marró. Brown eyes can be either: ulls marrons or ulls castanys.
  • Moreno covers both hair and skin. If you mean skin: té la pell fosca — dark skin, té la pell clara — fair skin.

Té els cabells llisos, però avui els porta recollits. — Her hair is straight, but today she's got it tied up.

Length can be in between: porta els cabells més aviat curts — her hair is shortish, rather short; more on these words in chapter 6.

3. Ros, rossa, rossos: adjectives with four forms

In English “tall” is always “tall”. In Catalan the adjective agrees with the person or thing in gender and number, so most descriptive words have four forms: masculine, feminine, masculine plural, feminine plural. The basic pattern is simple: feminine +a, plural +s / +es. But this unit gathers nearly all the awkward cases, so let's go through them.

m.f.m. pl.f. pl.
altaltaaltsaltes
primprimaprimsprimes
castanycastanyacastanyscastanyes

grasgrassagrassosgrasses
grisgrisagrisosgrises
nerviósnerviosanerviososnervioses
pèl-roigpèl-rojapèl-rojospèl-roges

blaublavablausblaves
tranquiltranquil·latranquilstranquil·les

What's going on:

  • Words in -s: double or single? Ros → rossa, gras → grassa, but gris → grisa, nerviós → nerviosa. It's about sound: a single s between vowels is voiced, [z] as in rose, while ss is [s] as in kiss. You hear [s] in rossa and [z] in grisa [ˈgɾizə]. It's the same reading rule as in casa and passar (introduction, chapter 1.2). Hence a pair that's easy to mix up by ear: rosa [z] — pink, rossa [s] — blonde. Masculine plural of words in -s ends in -os: rossos, grisos, nerviosos.
  • The end of a word is devoiced. Groc → groga (c → g), roig → roja → roges (ig → j → g), blau → blava (u → v). At the end of a word the consonant sounds voiceless (or turns into a “u”), but before a vowel you hear the voiced one: groc [ˈgɾɔk] — groga [ˈgɾɔɣə]. English doesn't do this — dog keeps its g — so listen for it (introduction, chapter 1.3). But blanc → blanques (c → qu before e) is spelling only: the sound stays [k].
  • Ela geminada. Tranquil → tranquil·la, intel·ligent: l·l is a long l, like the two l's in full-length or cool lake.
  • One form for both genders — many words ending in -e, all words in -ista and many in -ent/-ant: jove, gran, alegre, amable, xerraire, ximple, optimista, pessimista, intel·ligent, pacient, impacient. The plural just adds s: joves, optimistes, pacients. So un home optimista and una dona optimista — the -a here isn't feminine. There are plenty of exceptions, so check the dictionary: negre → negra, content → contenta, lent → lenta.

3.1 Diminutives: baixet, grassonet

The ending -et, -eta softens a judgement, a bit like English -ish or “a bit on the … side”: baix → baixet (shortish), prim → primet (slight, skinny), petit → petitet (tiny). Gras has a longer form: grassonet, grassoneta (plump, chubby). About looks, diminutives sound polite, so you hear them all the time.

Traps

La samarreta is a T-shirt; a shirt is la camisa. El vestit is a dress or a man's suit, never a vest: porta vestit i corbata — he's wearing a suit and tie. Les sabatilles are first of all slippers; trainers are les vambes, or in full les sabatilles esportives. Els mitjons are socks, les mitges are tights. And a skirt is la faldilla: the word falda in Catalan usually means your lap (el nen seu a la falda de la mare — the child is sitting on his mother's lap).

If you know Spanish

Most names are recognisable through Spanish, but watch these: samarreta is camiseta — don't say camiseta; Spanish zapatillas are vambes or sabatilles esportives (trainers) and sabatilles (slippers); vaqueros are texans, calcetines are mitjons, falda is faldilla.

  • Things that come in pairs are plural, as in English, and take uns, unes where English says “a pair of” or nothing: Porto uns texans i unes vambes. — I'm wearing jeans and trainers.
  • Material, pattern and style are added with de after the noun, where English puts a word before it: un jersei de llana (a woollen jumper), un jersei de coll alt (a polo neck, turtleneck), una camisa de ratlles (a striped shirt), una camisa de quadres (a checked shirt), una samarreta de màniga curta (a short-sleeved T-shirt), una samarreta sense mànigues (a sleeveless T-shirt), unes sabates de pell (leather shoes).
  • “Dressed in” — anar vestit, anar vestida, literally “go dressed”: Com vas vestida avui? — What are you wearing today? Va vestit de negre. — He's dressed in black.
  • “To put on” — posar-se: Què et poses per anar a treballar? — What do you put on for work? “To take off” — treure's: Em trec la jaqueta. — I take my jacket off. Note again la jaqueta — “the jacket”, not “my jacket”.

4.2 M'agrada el lila: your favourite colour

A colour used as a noun is masculine and takes the article: el lila, el verd, el taronja — where English just says “purple”, “green”. “To like” works back to front compared with English: m'agrada is literally “it pleases me”, so the verb agrees with the thing you like — m'agrada for one thing, m'agraden for several.

M'agrada el lila. — I like purple.
M'agraden els colors clars. — I like light colours.
M'agraden el verd i el rosa. — I like green and pink (two of them, so agraden).
Quin color t'agrada més? — El blau. — Which colour do you like best? — Blue.
A mi, el color que més m'agrada és el groc. — A mi, també. — The colour I like most is yellow. — Me too.

A few more useful words: xerraire — chatty, talkative (one form for both genders); bromista — a joker, likes a laugh; amable — kind, polite; aplicat, aplicada — studious, diligent (of a pupil); inquiet, inquieta — restless, fidgety; esportista — sporty.

Note that optimista and pessimista are adjectives: Soc optimista — I'm optimistic, with no “an”.

False friends

Simpàtic is not “sympathetic” (understanding, compassionate): it means nice, pleasant to be around, likeable. Ximple is not “simple” in the sense of “easy, plain” (that's senzill): it means silly. Discret is a compliment — someone who doesn't gossip or say too much. Tafaner is its opposite, someone who pokes their nose into other people's business. And és nerviós describes a highly strung, restless person, while està nerviós is “he is nervous” right now, as in English (see 1.2 on ser and estar).

Asking about someone's character: Com és el teu germà? — What's your brother like? The same question works for looks; if you specifically mean looks, add: Com és físicament? — What does he look like?

6. Molt, força, una mica, no gens: how much

Saying “tall” is rarely enough — you usually want to say how tall. The scale from “very” to “not at all”:

CatalanEnglish
És molt alta.very tall
És força alta.quite tall, pretty tall
És bastant alta.quite tall, pretty tall
És més aviat alta.rather tall, on the tall side
És una mica tímida.a bit shy
No és gaire alta.not very tall
No és gens tímida.not shy at all
  • Força and bastant mean the same; força is very Catalan, you hear it all the time in Barcelona.
  • Una mica usually goes with negative traits: una mica tímid, una mica gandul, una mica grassonet. Just like “a bit” in English: “a bit lazy” is fine, “a bit clever” sounds odd.
  • Gaire and gens need no: No és gaire alt — he's not very tall (a softer way to say “short”). No és gens simpàtic — he's not nice at all. No és molt alt is also correct, but no és gaire alt sounds more natural.

For English speakers

The negative comes in two pieces: no before the verb and gaire / gens after it: No + és + gaire / gens + adjective — “not is very…”, “not is at all…”. Without the no it's wrong: És gens tímida. It's the same pattern as no… mai in lesson 5.

6.1 No, no ho és: ho instead of the adjective

English short answers echo the verb: “Is he tall? — No, he isn't.” Catalan does something similar, but adds ho (“it, so”) in place of the adjective you don't want to repeat:

És alt en Jordi? — No, no ho és. — Is Jordi tall? — No, he isn't.
I en Pol? — Sí, sí que ho és. — And Pol? — Oh yes, he is.
Anna, ets xerraire? — No, no ho soc gens. — Anna, are you chatty? — No, not at all.
Estàs cansat? — Sí, ho estic. — Are you tired? — Yes, I am.

  • Ho goes right before the verb: no ho és, ho soc. Where English echoes just the verb (“yes, I am”), Catalan sounds much more natural with ho: Sí, ho soc.
  • Sí que ho és is an emphatic yes: “oh yes, he is”, “he certainly is”. This sí que is very common in answers.
  • A short answer without the verb also works: És alta? — No gaire. — Not very.

If you know Spanish

Ho here is Spanish lo in no lo es. The degree words: força = bastante, més aviat = más bien, no gaire = no muy, no gens = nada.

7. Més alt que, tan alta com: comparing

PatternExampleEnglish
més … queEn Jordi és més alt que les seves germanes.Jordi is taller than his sisters.
menys … queEn Pau és menys tímid que la seva germana.Pau is less shy than his sister.
tan … comLa Núria és tan baixa com la seva mare.Núria is as short as her mother.
  • There's no -er ending: “taller” is always més alt, “shorter” — més baix, “happier” — més content. “Than” is que, and “as … as” is tan … com.
  • After que and com you use jo and tu — the subject forms, where English says “me”, “you”: És més alt que jo. — He's taller than me. Soc tan pacient com tu. — I'm as patient as you.
  • Més gran about people means “older”, not “bigger”; més petit — “younger”: La meva germana és més gran que jo. — My sister is older than me. “Younger” can also be més jove: En Pol és més jove que el seu cosí.
  • Irregular forms, as in English: millor (better) and pitjor (worse): Aquesta foto és millor que l'altra. — This photo is better than the other one.
  • “The tallest”, “the most…” — el més, la més, and “in” the group is de: L'Anna és la més alta de la classe. — Anna is the tallest in the class.

Tan and tant

Before an adjective — tan: tan alt com — as tall as. With a verb — tant: No treballa tant com tu. — He doesn't work as much as you. The same split as English “as” and “as much”.
