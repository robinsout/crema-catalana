1. Jobs: masculine and feminine

Many job titles look familiar through English (mecànic — mechanic, pintor — painter, professor). The new part is gender: English says a nurse for a man or a woman, but Catalan usually has two forms, and the feminine is built in several ways. Learn the masculine ending — it usually tells you what the pair will be.

Pronunciation: the silent r

In the masculine -er, the final r is silent: cambrer [kəmˈbɾe], fuster [fusˈte], cuiner [kujˈne] — a bit like British -er in teacher, where the r isn't heard either, but the vowel stays a clear, stressed [e], not the weak sound at the end of teacher. In the feminine it comes back: cambrera [kəmˈbɾeɾə]. Metge is [ˈmed͡ʒə]: tg sounds like the dg in bridge (introductory lesson, chapter 1.2).

False friends

La dependenta is a shop assistant, not a “dependant”. El fuster is a carpenter, from la fusta — wood as a material. El mestre teaches children at primary school, while el professor teaches at secondary school, university and on courses — not only a “professor”: your Catalan teacher is a professor or professora.

If you know Spanish

Most job titles are close to Spanish, with a few traps: el lampista is fontanero (and often electricista), el fuster is carpintero, and metgessa has -essa where Spanish just says médica.

2. De què treballes? — treballar de, fer de, ser

There are several ways to ask about someone's job, and you'll hear all of them:

De què treballes? — What do you do? (literally “you work of what?”)
A què et dediques? — What do you do for a living?
Treballes? — Do you work? / Are you working?
On treballes? — Where do you work?

Note the questions have no do: the verb form treballes (“you work”) already says who.

And three ways to answer:

StructureExampleNote
treballar de + jobTreballo de cambrer.“I work as a waiter”: the most common answer to De què treballes?
fer de + jobFaig de dependenta.“I work as / I'm doing the job of”: often a temporary or side job
ser + jobSoc infermera.“I'm a nurse”: your profession as who you are
  • No “a” in any of the three. English says I'm a doctor, I work as a waiter; Catalan says Soc metge, not Soc un metge, and Treballo de cambrer. The article appears only when the job has a description: És una metgessa molt bona. — She's a very good doctor.
  • “As a” is de after treballar and fer. You can also say Treballo com a cambrer — “I work as a waiter”, but never com cambrer: “as a” in this sense is com a.
  • For students it's only ser: Soc estudiant. A student studies — treballar doesn't fit.

For English speakers

If you catch yourself starting with “I'm a…”, drop the a: Soc professora. If you start with “I work as a…”, turn as a into de: Treballo de cuiner. Same in the third person: La Marta és advocada — Marta is a lawyer.

3. Where I work: a, en, a la

English switches between in and at: in Sabadell, at the chemist's, in a bar. Catalan has its own rule — the preposition depends on what comes after it:

What followsPrepositionExample
a town, a countryaTreballo a Sabadell. Treballa a Alemanya.
the definite articlea la, a l', al, alsTreballo a la farmàcia. Treballa al mercat.
un, unaen (or a)Treballo en una fàbrica. Treballa en un bar.
homea casaTreballo a casa.
  • a + el = al: al taller, al bar de la cantonada (the bar on the corner); a + els = als: als Estats Units. No merging with la and l': a la fleca, a l'hospital.
  • Before un, una both en and a are correct; in Barcelona you'll mostly hear en: en una oficina, en un restaurant.
  • With a named place, use the article and a: Treballo a la llibreria Laie, a l'Hospital de Sant Pau.
  • A casa has no article and no “my”: “I work at home / from home” — Treballo a casa.

If you know Spanish

Spanish uses en almost everywhere: trabajo en Barcelona, en la farmacia. Don't carry it over: Treballo en Barcelona, en la farmàcia are typical mistakes. With a town and with el, la it's always a.

False friends

Rentar is “to wash”, not “to rent” (that's llogar). Despatxar in a shop means “to serve”, not “to dispatch”; it can also mean “to fire”, but the usual word for firing someone is acomiadar. Portar is “to carry, bring”, not “to port”.

4.1 La feina and el treball

Both mean “work, job”, but when people talk about their own job they almost always say la feina:

Vaig a la feina amb metro. — I go to work by metro.
Avui tinc molta feina. — I've got a lot of work today.
Busco feina. — I'm looking for a job.
M'agrada la meva feina. — I like my job.

El treball is work in a more general or official sense: el lloc de treball (job, position), el món del treball (the world of work). It's also a piece of written work at school or university: un treball de recerca — a research project.

5. Hours, days off and colleagues

After “what” and “where” comes “when”: your hours, your days off. Times and days of the week are covered in detail in the unit 4 lesson, your daily routine in the unit 5 lesson. Here are the words you need specifically for work:

  • Fer festa (or tenir festa) means “to have the day off” — no party involved. A party is una festa, and “to go out partying” is sortir de festa.
  • El cap — boss (and also “head”): La meva cap és molt simpàtica. — My boss is very nice. Colleagues are els companys de feina.
  • El sou — salary, pay. Don't confuse it with sou — “you are” (vosaltres sou).

For English speakers

“From … to …” is de … a …: de nou a cinc, de dilluns a divendres, de setembre a juny. No “o'clock” — just the numbers; instead of am/pm, add del matí, de la tarda, del vespre when it isn't clear: de deu del vespre a vuit del matí. Des de (“starting from, since”) also exists, but for working hours de … a … is all you need.

6. Out of work, retired, studying

Not everyone in the class has a job, and that needs words too. For unemployment and retirement the verb is estar, the “to be” for states and situations: estic a l'atur, estic jubilat (Soc jubilat is also said). For studying it's ser: Soc estudiant.

Estic a l'atur. — I'm unemployed, out of work.
Busco feina de cuinera. — I'm looking for work as a cook.
Estic jubilat. / Estic jubilada. — I'm retired.
Soc estudiant. — I'm a student.
Faig pràctiques en una empresa. — I'm doing an internship at a company.
Ara no treballo: cuido els meus fills. — I'm not working at the moment: I look after my children.

  • A l'atur — with a and the article, not en l'atur. L'atur is also unemployment itself and the benefit: cobro l'atur — I'm on unemployment benefit.
  • Busco feina de… uses the same de as treballo de…, again with no “a”.
  • Jubilat means “retired” (think jubilee).
  • Catalonia's job centre is el SOC (Servei d'Ocupació de Catalunya); you register there when you lose your job.

If you know Spanish

Estic a l'atur is estoy en el paro — but with a, not en l'atur. And la feina looks like faena, but it's the everyday word for “work, job”.

6.1 A short conversation about work

A typical exchange — with someone who has a job and someone who's looking:

Treballes? — Sí, treballo de fuster en un taller de Gràcia. — Do you work? — Yes, I'm a carpenter in a workshop in Gràcia.
I t'agrada? — Sí, molt. Però plego tard. — And do you like it? — Very much. But I finish late.
I tu? — Jo ara estic a l'atur. Busco feina d'administrativa. — And you? — I'm out of work at the moment. I'm looking for an office job.

Plegar means “to finish work for the day”: A quina hora plegues? — What time do you finish?

When you talk about someone else, the verb goes into the third person and el meu, la meva (my) becomes el seu, la seva (his, her): La Marta treballa de fustera. Està molt contenta amb la seva feina. — Marta works as a carpenter. She's very happy with her job.

Write six to eight sentences: what you do and where (or what you study, or what job you're looking for), your hours, what you do at work and whether you like it. If you work remotely for a company back home, say so: Treballo des de casa per a una empresa britànica.

La meva dona és mestra: treballa en una escola del Clot i fa classes als nens de primer. Fa festa el cap de setmana, com jo.
