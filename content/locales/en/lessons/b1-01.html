1.1 “How are you?” and answers

Com estàs? / Com va? — How are you? / How’s it going?
Molt bé, i tu? — Very well, and you?
Bé, gràcies. — Fine, thanks. Anar fent. — Getting by.

Say it right

gràcies (thank you) is stressed on the first syllable, with an open a: [ˈgra-si-əs] — not “gra-SEE-as”.

1.2 Nice to meet you

Encantat! / Encantada! — Nice to meet you! (said by a man / a woman)
Molt de gust. — Nice to meet you (the same for everyone).
Igualment. — Likewise.

Something English never does: encantat changes with the person speaking. A woman says encantada, a man encantat. If you don’t want to think about it, use molt de gust.

2.1 Em dic — “I call myself”

Catalan doesn’t say “my name is”: it says em dic, literally “I call myself”, from dir-se (dir — to say). The little “myself” word goes before the verb and changes with the person:

dir-se
joem dic
tuet dius
ell, ella, vostèes diu
nosaltresens diem
vosaltresus dieu
ells, elles, vostèses diuen

Com es diu ell? — What’s his name? (literally “how does he call himself?”)

2.2 “Soc la Maria”: “the” before a name

The title of the unit shows the first surprise. In everyday speech, people’s names take an article, as if you said “I am the Maria”: la Maria, en Joan, and before a vowel l'Anna, l'Àlex.

  • en is the masculine article used only before people’s names, la the feminine one.
  • No article after em dic: Em dic Maria. But Soc la Maria., Aquest és en Joan.
  • Your name gets one too: en James, la Sarah, l'Emma.

Et presento en Joan. — This is Joan (introducing him to you).
Aquesta és la Laura, la meva companya. — This is Laura, my classmate.

2.3 From and in: de, a

Soc de Londres, soc d'Edimburg (before a vowel, d'). “To live in” is viure a: Visc a Gràcia. The “I” form of viure is irregular: visc.

Some countries take “the” in Catalan — like English the Netherlands or the USA, but for many more countries. Then de merges with the article: de + el = del.

Which countries take the article just has to be learnt. Common ones: el Marroc, el Perú, l'Equador, l'Argentina, el Brasil, el Canadà, el Senegal, el Japó, el Regne Unit, els Estats Units. So someone from the UK says Soc del Regne Unit, but Soc d'Escòcia.

2.4 Talking about someone else

In class you often introduce the person sitting next to you. It’s all the same, just in the he/she form:

Es diu Olena. — Her name is Olena.
És d'Ucraïna. És ucraïnesa. — She’s from Ukraine, she’s Ukrainian.
La seva llengua materna és l'ucraïnès. — Her mother tongue is Ukrainian.
Viu a Sants. — She lives in Sants.
El seu telèfon és el sis, u, zero… — Her phone number is 6, 1, 0…

If you don’t know: No ho sé. — I don’t know.

3. The verb ser and the subject pronouns

serdir-seviure
josocem dicvisc
tuetset diusvius

jo — I, tu — you, ell / ella — he / she, nosaltres — we, vosaltres — you (plural), ells / elles — they (men / women).

3.1 What works differently from English

  • The pronoun is usually dropped: the verb ending already says who. Soc anglès. — I’m English. Add jo only for contrast: Jo soc anglès, i ella és irlandesa.
  • Two words for “you”: tu for one person, vosaltres for several. The polite vostè (plural vostès) takes the he/she form of the verb: Vostè és la senyora Puig? In class and with people your age, everyone uses tu from day one.
  • “They” has a gender: ells for men or a mixed group, elles for women. But nosaltres and vosaltres stay the same for a group of women.
  • soc has no accent mark (old spelling: sóc), but és and són do: the mark tells “is / are” apart from es (himself) and son (sleep).

If you know Spanish

ser is ser, vostè is usted. But it’s em dic, not me dic, and there is no nosotras: a group of women is nosaltres too.

4.1 The language is the masculine nationality

l'anglès, el castellà, el català, el francès, l'alemany, el rus

Parlo anglès i una mica de castellà, i estudio català. — I speak English and a little Spanish, and I’m learning Catalan.

Castellà, not espanyol

In Catalonia the Spanish language is usually called el castellà. Espanyol is the nationality, or “Spanish” as an adjective.

4.2 Your mother tongue

In a class where everyone comes from somewhere else, this is one of the first questions:

Quina és la teva llengua materna? — What’s your mother tongue?
La meva llengua materna és l'anglès. — My mother tongue is English.
Les meves llengües maternes són l'anglès i el gal·lès. — My mother tongues are English and Welsh.

Unlike in English, the name of a language takes “the”: l'anglès, l'àrab, el romanès, el tagal. But after parlar the article is usually dropped: Parlo anglès.

5. My, your, his, her: possessives with “the”

“My phone number”, “your mother tongue”, “her surname” — you need them on day one. Catalan possessives come with the article: literally “the my phone”.

m.f.m. pl.f. pl.
myel meula mevaels meusles meves
yourel teula tevaels teusles teves
his, her, your (vostè)el seula sevaels seusles seves

el meu telèfon, el meu cognom — my phone number, my surname (cognom is masculine!)
la teva llengua materna — your mother tongue (llengua is feminine)
el seu nom — his name / her name

  • The form agrees with the thing owned, not with the owner — English has nothing like it. A man says la meva llengua, a woman says el meu cognom.
  • So el seu doesn’t tell his from her — and it is also the polite your with vostè: Quin és el seu telèfon? — What’s his / her / your phone number?
  • “Which / what” has a gender too: Quin és el teu telèfon? but Quina és la teva llengua materna?

If you know Spanish

Not mi telèfon but el meu telèfon: the Spanish mi doesn’t exist in modern Catalan.

6.2 Spelling an English name to a Catalan

English names are full of letters and letter pairs that Catalan uses differently. Spell them letter by letter, and expect them to be read the Catalan way:

Wve doblea b or a u sound: William → “Biliam”
Yi gregaan i: Lynn → “Linn”

th, shtwo letters: te, hac; essa, hact and s with a silent h — these sounds don’t exist in Catalan spelling; sh is written x in Catalan

So don’t be surprised if your name sounds different in a Catalan mouth — it’s the Catalan reading of the letters, not a mistake.

7. Numbers 0–20 for forms and phone numbers

zero, u, dos, tres, quatre, cinc, sis, set, vuit, nou, deu
onze, dotze, tretze, catorze, quinze, setze, disset, divuit, dinou, vint

  • u is used when counting and in phone numbers, un / una before a noun; dos / dues change with the gender: dues germanes (two sisters).
  • Phone numbers are given digit by digit, with el (“the”) before the number: Quin és el teu telèfon? — El meu telèfon és el sis, quatre, u, zero, set, vuit, dos, nou, cinc. Digits 0–9 are enough, but say them fast and don’t mix up set (7) and sis (6). And zero is zero, never “oh”.
  • Quants anys tens? — How old are you? Tinc vint anys. — I’m twenty (literally “I have twenty years”).

All the numbers, the time and the calendar in detail: the lesson Els nombres i el calendari.

Mission

Introduce yourself in Catalan to a Catalan friend or neighbour, and ask them to spell their surname: M'ho pots lletrejar? Write it down and check you understood the letter names.
