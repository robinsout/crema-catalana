1. Sounds

1.1 Vowels: seven when stressed, three when not

Catalan has 7 vowel sounds in stressed syllables and only 3 in unstressed ones. The good news: the main unstressed vowel is one you already use all the time in English.

StressedExampleSound
acasa (house)[a] as in father, but shorter
è — opencel (sky), cafè[ɛ] as in bed
é — closedmés (more)[e] as in say, without the glide
ivi (wine)[i] as in see, short
ò — openporta (door), dona (woman)[ɔ] as in British hot
ó — closedtot (all), són (they are)[o] as in go, without the glide
uun (one)[u] as in food, short

The accent mark isn’t always written (cel and porta have none), so whether an e or o is open or closed is often something you learn with the word.

Pure vowels

English speakers tend to turn long vowels into glides: no sounds like “noh-oo”, say like “seh-ee”. Catalan vowels stay steady from start to finish: no is a short, flat [no], and més is [mes], not “mace”.

Unstressed vowels shrink, much as they do in English:

  • unstressed a and e become the neutral sound [ə] — the first vowel of about, the last of sofa: casa [ˈka-zə], Barcelona [bər-sə-ˈlo-nə], pare (father) [ˈpa-rə]
  • unstressed o becomes [u]: porta [ˈpɔr-tə] → portar (to carry) [pur-ˈta], Montserrat [mun-sə-ˈrat]

Your advantage

You already reduce vowels: the two a’s in banana that aren’t stressed are both [ə]. Catalan does the same thing — just don’t read the word letter by letter.

1.3 Silent and devoiced final consonants

  • The -r of infinitives is silent: parlar (to speak) [pərˈla], fer (to do) [fe], dormir (to sleep) [durˈmi]. So is the -r of many other words: carrer (street) [kəˈre], senyor [səˈɲo].
  • Final -nt, -lt, -mp, -mb: the last consonant is silent. cent (hundred) [sen], molt (very) [mol], camp (field) [kam], amb (with) [am].
  • Devoicing at the end of a word — something English doesn’t do: English keeps bed and bet apart, Catalan pronounces a final d as t. verd (green) [bɛrt], fred (cold) [frɛt].

1.4 Stress

  • Words ending in a vowel, vowel + s, -en or -in are stressed on the second-to-last syllable: ca·sa, par·len, jo·ves.
  • All the others on the last one: par·lar, ciu·tat, a·ni·mal.
  • Any word that breaks this rule gets a written accent (see section 2).

2. Spelling

2.1 Two accent marks

  • Grave (`) — open sound: à, è, ò
  • Acute (´) — closed sound: é, í, ó, ú

So a always takes à, and i/u always take í/ú. But e/o can take either, and the mark tells you how to say them: cafè, francès [ɛ], but també, més [e]; còpia [ɔ], but córrer [o].

When to write an accent:

  • stress on the last syllable, and the word ends in a vowel, vowel + s, -en or -in: català, cafè, també, ningú, anglès, convé;
  • stress on the second-to-last syllable, and the word ends in anything else: fàcil, difícil, àngel, córrer (compare llapis — no accent);
  • stress on the third-to-last syllable — always: música, pàgina, telèfon.

2.2 Accents that tell words apart

Old spelling

Older textbooks and shop signs still have sóc, dóna, véns, vénen, ós. Today the correct spelling is soc, dona, vens, venen, os.

2.3 The diaeresis

  • ü in güe/güi/qüe/qüi shows that the u is pronounced: aigüera (sink), qüestió, pingüí.
  • ï / ü splits two vowels into separate syllables, as in naïve: veïna [bə-ˈi-nə] (neighbour), raïm (grapes), països (countries).

2.4 The apostrophe and contractions

As in English contractions, the apostrophe replaces a dropped vowel — but in Catalan it is compulsory:

  • el / la (the) → l' before a vowel or h: l'home (the man), l'aigua (the water), l'escola (the school). But la stays whole before an unstressed i/u: la universitat, la història.
  • de (of, from) → d'; em / et / es → m' / t' / s': m'agrada (I like it).

Some prepositions merge with the article, always:

elels
aalals
dedeldels
perpelpels

Vaig al mercat. — I’m going to the market. Vinc del gimnàs. — I’m coming from the gym.

2.5 “The” before names

In everyday speech people’s first names take an article, as if you said “the Joan”: en Joan, l'Albert, la Maria, l'Anna.

Has vist en Pere? — Have you seen Pere?

3. Grammar: what works differently from English

3.1 Every noun has a gender

Every noun is masculine or feminine, even for things, and the article and adjectives agree with it:

masculinefeminine
the (singular)el llibre (the book)la casa (the house)
the (plural)els llibresles cases
a, anun llibreuna casa
adjectiveun pis petit (a small flat)una casa petita

Most nouns ending in -a are feminine, but not all (el dia, el mapa), and many have no telltale ending (la llet — milk, el pa — bread). Learn every noun with its article: not “llet” but la llet.

3.2 The verb ending already says who

Each verb has a different form for each person, so the subject pronoun is usually left out:

Parlo català. — I speak Catalan. Vivim a Gràcia. — We live in Gràcia.

Adding jo (I), tu (you)… is correct but sounds emphatic: Jo parlo català, ell no. — I speak Catalan, he doesn’t.

3.3 Two verbs for “to be”: ser and estar

  • ser — what something is, and where it is: Soc anglès. — I’m English. On és el bany? — Where is the toilet?
  • estar — how someone is right now: Estic cansat. — I’m tired. La sopa està freda. — The soup is cold.
  • estar also means “to stay, to spend time”: Vaig estar dues setmanes a Roma. — I spent two weeks in Rome.

3.4 “Have” where English says “be”

EnglishCatalanLiterally
I’m 30.Tinc trenta anys.I have 30 years.
I’m hungry.Tinc gana.I have hunger.
I’m cold.Tinc fred.I have cold.
It’s cold (weather).Fa fred.It makes cold.

3.5 “Vaig + infinitive” is the past, not the future

It looks like “I go to…”, but it is the everyday past tense:

Vaig parlar amb ell ahir. — I spoke to him yesterday.
Ahir vam anar a la platja. — Yesterday we went to the beach.

The helper verb goes vaig, vas, va, vam, vau, van + infinitive. To say “I’m going to eat”, use the future: Menjaré ara.

3.6 “The my house”: possessives take an article

el meu cotxe (my car), la meva casa (my house), els teus amics (your friends), les seves idees (his/her/their ideas). The possessive agrees with the thing owned, not with the owner: la seva can mean “his”, “her” or “their”.

3.7 Questions and “no”

  • No do: a question is a statement with rising intonation. Parles anglès? — Do you speak English?
  • no goes before the verb: No parlo alemany. — I don’t speak German.
  • Double negatives are normal: No ve ningú. — Nobody is coming (literally “not comes nobody”). No vull res. — I don’t want anything. No hi he estat mai. — I’ve never been there.
  • pas adds emphasis: No vull pas anar-hi. — I really don’t want to go.

3.8 en and hi: words English doesn’t have

  • en — “some, of it, of them”: Vols pa? — Sí, en vull. (Do you want bread? — Yes, I want some.)
  • hi — “there”: Vas a Girona? — Sí, hi vaig. (Are you going to Girona? — Yes, I’m going there.)
  • hi ha — “there is, there are”: Hi ha un problema.

These small words (pronoms febles, weak pronouns) go before the verb and are one of the hardest parts of Catalan; they get a lesson of their own.

If you know Spanish

Spanish helps with vocabulary, but its habits creep in. Not tinc que but he de; not mi casa but la meva casa; “where is” is on és, not on està; and vaig + infinitive is the past, not “voy a”.

Group III. Most -ir verbs add -eix- in the singular and in “they”. For example: servir, llegir (to read), preferir, patir (to suffer), decidir, construir, oferir (to offer).

The “pure” ones are few, so learn them as a list: dormir, sortir, obrir, sentir, collir, cosir, fugir, morir, omplir, tossir, bullir, cobrir, escopir.

Two kinds of “you”

tu is the everyday “you” for one person, vosaltres for several. The polite vostè / vostès takes the he/she/they form of the verb: Vostè parla anglès? — Do you speak English?

5. Irregular verbs and their patterns

Irregular verbs in Catalan aren’t random: they follow a few recognisable patterns.

5.1 A -c in the “I” form

Many group II verbs and some group III verbs end in -c in the jo form:

Tip

This c/g carries on into the subjunctive (tingui, pugui, vingui) and often into a participle in -gut.

5.2 The -eix- verbs

For -ir verbs, adding -eix- is the default, not the exception. The exceptions are the “pure” verbs listed in section 4.2.

5.5 Verbs whose stem changes

  • anar (to go): vaig / anem, future aniré
  • sortir (to go out): surto / sortim
  • cosir, tossir (to sew, to cough): cuso / cosim, tusso / tossim. An unstressed o already sounds like [u]; under stress the spelling shows it.
  • ser (to be): future seré, imperfet era, subjunctive sigui

6. Practice

6.1 Read aloud

Watch out for the shrinking vowels and the silent letters.

Bon dia! Em dic Àlex i visc a Barcelona. Soc anglès, però parlo una mica de castellà. Fa poc que estudio català. Ahir vaig anar a classe i vaig conèixer molta gent interessant. Cada matí em llevo d'hora i esmorzo al bar de la cantonada.
Translation

Good morning! My name is Àlex and I live in Barcelona. I’m English, but I speak a little Spanish. I started learning Catalan recently. Yesterday I went to class and met lots of interesting people. Every morning I get up early and have breakfast at the bar on the corner.

6.2 Conjugate in the present tense

  1. cantar (to sing)
  2. llegir (to read) — with -eix- or “pure”?
  3. sortir (to go out) — with -eix- or “pure”?
  4. menjar (to eat) — watch the spelling in nosaltres and vosaltres
Answers
  1. canto, cantes, canta, cantem, canteu, canten
  2. llegeixo, llegeixes, llegeix, llegim, llegiu, llegeixen — with -eix-
  3. surto, surts, surt, sortim, sortiu, surten — “pure”, and o changes to u
  4. menjo, menges, menja, mengem, mengeu, mengen

6.3 Translate with “vaig + infinitive”

  1. Yesterday I worked until eight.
  2. We had dinner with friends.
  3. They went to the cinema.
Answers
  1. Ahir vaig treballar fins a les vuit.
  2. Vam sopar amb els amics.
  3. Van anar al cinema.
